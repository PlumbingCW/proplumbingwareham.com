Sewer Inspection in Wareham, MA

A sewer inspection sends a waterproof camera down the buried pipe between your house and the town main or septic tank, so you can see its real condition on a screen. It is the planned way to learn about roots, cracks, bellies and offsets before they turn into a backup.

We book camera visits for home buyers, owners of older houses and anyone with a line that keeps slowing down. You get a clear price before the camera goes in, and a plain explanation of what the footage shows.

What a sewer camera inspection shows

The inspection uses a push cable with a color camera head and built-in lights, fed into the line through a cleanout. As the head moves, it records the pipe wall, the joints and any water pooling in low spots. A locator at the surface can pick up the camera transmitter, so a problem 40 feet in can be marked above ground.

You see the footage as it happens, and we explain the findings in plain terms. A pipe that looks clean needs nothing more. A pipe with roots or a crack gets an honest recommendation, which might be sewer line cleaning, a sewer line repair or simply a note to watch it.

How a visit works from start to finish

A routine inspection takes a short visit, and you do not need to be present for the whole thing.

  1. 1

    Find the access point

    We look for a cleanout near the foundation or in the basement. When none exists, the camera can go in through a toilet flange or a roof vent.

  2. 2

    Clear what is in the way

    If grease, paper or roots block the view, we may need to run a cable first. We tell you before doing so, because it changes the scope.

  3. 3

    Record the run

    The camera travels the full length it can reach, marking depth and distance on the video.

  4. 4

    Review and decide

    We walk through the footage with you and explain what, if anything, needs action. You decide what happens next.

When a planned camera inspection makes sense

This page is for planned work. If sewage is already backing up, call the 24/7 line and the dispatcher books the next open plumber. For everything else, these are the usual reasons to book.

  • ✓You are buying an older house and want to know the condition of the lateral before closing, not after.
  • ✓Drains in the home clog repeatedly even after cable cleaning, which suggests a damaged or sagging pipe.
  • ✓You plan a renovation, a new bathroom or an addition that will add load to the existing line.
  • ✓A large tree stands near the line, and you want to know if roots have entered a joint.
  • ✓You see damp or sunken areas on the lawn, or notice a faint sewage smell in the yard.
  • ✓A past repair was done, and you want proof that the new section is sound.

A plumber's camera is not a Title 5 inspection

Massachusetts requires a Title 5 inspection when a property with a septic system is sold. The rule is 310 CMR 15.300 to 15.305, the inspection is due within two years before or six months after the transfer, and only MassDEP-approved inspectors can perform it.

Our camera inspection is a different thing. It shows the condition of the pipe from the house toward the tank or main, and it can flag a cracked line, but it does not take the place of a Title 5 report and does not produce one. If you are selling or buying a septic property, you still need the approved inspector.

Buying a house in Wareham: why the pipe matters

Wareham's housing plan puts the median home at about 1969 construction, and roughly 20 percent of its housing predates 1939. A lateral from that era may be clay, cast iron or fiber pipe, and a standard home inspection will not usually put a camera in it.

The plan also calls out a large seasonal housing stock. A cottage that sat unused for months may show nothing wrong until the first full season of use. A camera visit before the purchase gives you a record of the pipe that you can use to negotiate a repair or plan a replacement.

Related work and nearby towns

If the camera shows only a clogged line, drain cleaning or sewer line cleaning may be all that you need. The same crews serve Marion, where roughly a third of developed properties are on septic, and Middleborough, where public sewer covers the center of town.

Where permits come into an inspection

A camera inspection is a look, not a repair, so it needs no permit. If the footage leads to replacing pipe, 248 CMR 3.05 requires a permit issued to a licensed individual before the work begins, and that is also the point where the town inspector gets involved.
