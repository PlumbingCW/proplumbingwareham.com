What Is a Non-Condensing Tankless Water Heater? A Wareham Guide

A non-condensing tankless water heater heats water on demand with a single heat exchanger and sends its exhaust out hot, as vapor. Because that exhaust stays hot and does not turn to liquid inside the unit, it has to leave the house through stainless steel venting rather than plastic pipe.

A condensing tankless unit adds a second heat exchanger that pulls more heat from the exhaust until it cools enough to form water, which is why it can use plastic vent pipe and needs a drain. Which one suits your house comes down to venting, gas supply and how much you value a higher efficiency rating.

  • Hot exhaust, stainless vent
  • No condensate drain
  • Simpler heat exchanger
  • Gas line must be sized
By the Wareham Pro Plumbing team | Published and updated October 24, 2026

Non-condensing versus condensing, side by side

The difference is what happens to the exhaust, and nearly everything else follows from that.

Non-condensing

  • One heat exchanger, exhaust leaves hot
  • Vents through stainless steel, usually a double-wall or single-wall stainless system
  • No condensate to drain
  • Lower efficiency rating and lower unit cost

Condensing

  • A second heat exchanger captures more heat from the exhaust
  • Exhaust is cool enough to vent in PVC, CPVC or polypropylene
  • Needs a condensate drain and often a neutralizer, since the liquid is acidic
  • Higher efficiency rating and a higher unit cost

What the efficiency trade-off means in plain words

Efficiency ratings for tankless units are published by the manufacturer as a UEF number, and condensing models rate higher because they recover heat that a non-condensing unit sends outdoors. Look at the label for the actual figure on the model you are quoted, since it changes by brand and size.

In practice, a household that runs a lot of hot water every day gets more back from a condensing unit over time. A household with lighter use, or one that is swapping out an old stainless vent that still works, may find a non-condensing unit the sensible fit. Neither type is right for everybody.

What a non-condensing install needs

Four things have to line up before the unit goes on the wall.

Stainless ventingHot exhaust must go through stainless pipe rated for the unit, sealed at the joints and sloped as the manufacturer specifies. Using PVC on a non-condensing unit is a fire and exhaust hazard.
A properly sized gas lineMany whole-house tankless units draw well over twice the gas of a typical gas tank. A line sized for the old tank can leave the new unit starved, and then it shuts down or fires poorly.
Combustion airThe unit burns a lot of fuel quickly and needs enough air, either from the room or through a sealed intake.
Water connectionsIsolation valves with service ports let the unit be flushed later. A pressure relief valve and discharge pipe are still required.

Condensing models share the gas and water needs but swap the stainless vent for plastic and add the drain, so the vent route and drain location drive the choice.

How a plumber sizes the gas line and vent

Skipping this part is how a new unit ends up underperforming.

  1. 1

    Read the input rating

    The unit's nameplate lists the maximum gas input in BTU per hour. That number sets the required flow.

  2. 2

    Measure the run

    The length of pipe from the meter or tank to the unit, plus the elbows and tees, changes how much gas gets through.

  3. 3

    Compare against the sizing table

    The plumber uses the gas piping tables adopted in the Massachusetts Fuel Gas and Plumbing Code, 248 CMR 10.00, for the fuel and pipe material in use.

  4. 4

    Check the meter or regulator

    A meter that fed a tank water heater and a furnace may not carry the added load. The gas company has to confirm that.

  5. 5

    Plan the vent route

    The termination must clear windows, doors and meter areas by the distances the manufacturer lists.

  6. 6

    Permit and test

    The licensed plumber pulls the permit under 248 CMR 3.05, tests the gas connection for leaks and has the inspector sign off.

Descaling is not optional on tankless

A tankless unit pushes water through narrow passages in the heat exchanger, and scale builds there over time. That shows up as longer waits, temperature swings and error codes. It is not a defect. It is how mineral-rich water behaves when heated fast.

Flushing is done by closing the isolation valves, connecting a small pump and hoses, and circulating a descaling solution through the unit, then rinsing. Intervals depend on your water and the manufacturer's manual. A unit that skips descaling for years can lose output or fail early, and a warranty may require the maintenance.

Wareham water and what it means for a tankless unit

The Wareham Fire District supplies water from seven gravel-packed wells in the Plymouth-Carver sole source aquifer and adds lime for corrosion control and pH. The Onset Fire District uses four groundwater wells and adjusts pH with sodium hydroxide. Both districts publish annual water quality reports, and neither page used for this guide lists a mineral level.

So read your own district's report and judge by what your fixtures show. Scale on the aerator or a kettle suggests the heat exchanger will see it too, and a descaling schedule should be set from there.

Flow rate and winter water temperature

A tankless unit is rated by how many gallons per minute it can heat by a given temperature rise. Cold incoming water in a Massachusetts winter is much colder than in August, so a unit that keeps up in summer may fall short when the shower, dishwasher and washer all run in January.

A plumber adds up the fixtures that run at the same time, picks a unit with margin for the coldest months, and explains what happens at the limit. When demand exceeds the rating, the water simply turns lukewarm rather than the unit failing. Two smaller units, or a tank for the busiest bathroom, are sometimes the better layout for a big household.

Should you stay with a tank?

A tankless unit saves floor space and gives a steady supply of hot water, but it does cost more to install, and it needs yearly attention. If your gas line is undersized or the vent route is awkward, a tank or a water heater installation with a power vent may be the cleaner answer.

What to do before you choose a non-condensing unit

Safety and venting come first with any gas appliance, so check these before you shop.

  • ✓Find out what kind of vent the unit needs and where it can safely exit the house.
  • ✓Make sure carbon monoxide alarms near the bedrooms and the utility area work.
  • ✓Never run a gas appliance with a blocked, loose or damaged vent.
  • ✓If you smell gas, leave the house and call the gas utility or 911 before you call a plumber.
