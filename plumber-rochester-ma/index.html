From the Town Common to the bogs: how Rochester is laid out

Town Hall at 1 Constitution Way sits on the Town Common along Route 105, and the rest of Rochester fans out from there. The town's own list of localities includes Bisbee Corner, East Rochester, North Rochester and Varella Corner, and those names are what most residents give when they describe where they live.

That matters on a call. Give the dispatcher your road plus the nearest corner, and we plan the drive from Wareham before a plumber heads out. Emergency dispatch is available around the clock to every part of town, nights and holidays included.

Water that belongs to more than one town

Rochester's hazard plan says the town relies on private wells and septic systems, and the aquifers under it also feed Marion, Mattapoisett and Fairhaven. Marion's municipal wells draw from Rochester ground, so in a dry summer the local water table can sink further than a homeowner expects.

If your taps sputter in August and the pressure tank and pump check out, the cause may be the aquifer, not your plumbing. A plumber can rule the pump and tank in or out by phone, which is worth knowing before you pay for a well contractor.

Two kinds of Rochester house, two kinds of emergency

Most calls fall into one of two groups, and the first question we ask sorts them.

Houses on a private well

  • Dead taps in a power cut because the pump stops
  • A pressure tank that short-cycles or loses its air charge
  • Water treatment gear plumbed into the pump line
  • A frozen line from the well house to the cellar

Houses with a failed supply or drain

  • A burst pipe in a closed-up second floor or garage
  • A water heater leaking at the base or the relief valve
  • Sinks and tubs backing up to a septic tank or distribution box
  • A slow leak that shows up as a wet wall or a high bill

Before we arrive: if you have a burst pipe or active leak

Rochester homes sit far apart, so the minutes before a plumber reaches you count. These four steps limit the damage.

Close the main

On a private well, the main shutoff is usually by the pressure tank. Close it fully, then open the lowest faucet to let the pipes empty.

Kill the well pump

Switch off the pump breaker so the tank stops pushing water at a split line. Go to the panel only when the floor around it is dry.

Clear the area

Keep people and pets away from standing water near outlets, a furnace or the water heater. Lift stored boxes and rugs off wet floors.

Call the 24/7 line

Give the dispatcher your road, your nearest corner and what failed. If the road has a gate or a long drive, say so.

When a storm cuts the power for days

The town's resilience report notes that nearly all power lines run overhead and falling trees have caused long outages. On a well, an outage is also a water outage. Work through this list.

  1. 1

    Fill containers early

    Fill tubs, buckets and the kettle before the pump stops, since the pressure tank holds only a short supply.

  2. 2

    Open one faucet

    Leave a cold tap open a crack in freezing weather so water keeps moving, and drain exposed lines if the heat is out for a long stretch.

  3. 3

    Use a generator safely

    Run it outside, away from windows, and never feed the house through an outlet. A transfer switch installed by an electrician is the safe way.

  4. 4

    Check when power returns

    Watch for pump short-cycling, a leak at the tank or a heater that will not relight, and call if any appear.

Bog country and a high water table

About 5 percent of the town is cranberry bog, and the hazard plan describes a high water table. Homes near low ground, ponds or bogs see damp cellars after long rain.

Water heaters and the trip to the cellar

When a tank heater fails in a house with a well, the first sign is often a puddle under it and a wet cellar floor. Water heater repair handles a bad relief valve, a leaking fitting or a failed element. A tank that has rusted through needs a replacement instead.

Water drawn from a well can carry sediment into the tank. A heater that rumbles or runs out of hot water early may be coated at the bottom, and a plumber can tell you if flushing will help or the tank is finished.

Permits in Rochester

Rochester's Building Department lists electrical, gas and plumbing permits and inspection scheduling among its services, and the Building Commissioner enforces the state code and the town bylaws. Permit rules vary, so ask the town before a heater swap or a remodel.

A plumber working under permit pulls it, and a leak repair on an existing line generally does not require one. Town Hall is on Route 105 if you prefer to ask in person.

What the state code says about permits

Rochester's building officials handle permits, and the rule they apply is 248 CMR 3.05. Stoppages and leaks inside a faucet or valve are exempt. A replaced water heater, a new pressure tank or a section of new pipe needs a permit that a licensed plumber pulls first.
