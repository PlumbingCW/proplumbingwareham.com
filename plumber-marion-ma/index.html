Marion's house eras decide what fails first

Marion began as a summer town in the 1870s, and after about 1880 large Shingle Style and Colonial Revival estates went up at Great Hill, Sippican Neck and along Water Street. Houses that old have been re-plumbed in pieces over a century, so a single wall can hold copper, galvanized steel and plastic in three different eras. Where those metals meet, joints leak first.

That history is why emergency dispatch available around the clock matters here. The town's own housing plan, using 2000 Census counts, found about half of owner-occupied homes were built before 1970, and a seasonal share of roughly 14 percent. An old supply line in a closed cottage can fail with nobody home, so the first call is often from a neighbor or the fire department.

Before we arrive: if you have a burst pipe or active leak in Marion

Marion's public water comes through the Marion Water Division, so most homes have a municipal shutoff at the street as well as one inside. These four moves limit damage while the 24/7 line gets a plumber headed your way.

Shut the main valve

Look in the basement, utility closet or crawlspace where the line enters, usually next to the meter. Turn the handle a quarter turn on a ball valve or clockwise on an old gate valve. If it will not move, do not force it, tell the dispatcher.

Kill power near wet areas

If water is dripping near the panel, outlets or a light fixture, shut off those breakers only if the floor around the panel is dry. Otherwise stay out and say so on the call.

Clear the area

Move rugs, boxes and electronics, and open a faucet at the lowest point to drain the line once the main is off. Keep children and pets away from standing water.

Call the 24/7 line

Give your street, say if you are on sewer or septic, and say where the water is. If you are on septic, say so at once, because a backup changes what the first plumber brings.

Flood-zone streets and wet basements

Much of Marion's dense downtown sits in a FEMA flood zone, and the town's hazard report flags Front Street, Silver Shell Beach, the Planting Island causeway, Converse Road, Point Road, Creek Road and East Marion. The same report says Hurricane Bob flooded the Town Hall basement in 1991. If your basement is on a street like these, groundwater is a regular visitor, not a rare one.

Sump pump quits in a storm

A pump that runs on every heavy rain can burn out or stick its float. A backup pump and a checked discharge line are the fix, and sump pump service covers both.

Septic in saturated soil

The town's report notes septic systems are at risk when the water table rises in floods. Slow drains across the whole house after storms point to the tank or leach field, not one fixture.

Salt air on fittings

Homes close to Sippican Harbor and Aucoot Creek see faster corrosion on exposed fittings, valves and the water heater flue. Rust at a shutoff is an early warning.

Power cuts after wind

Shallow-rooted pines come down in storms and take out power. A sump pump with no battery backup stops working exactly when the basement needs it.

Marion water: wells, iron and what comes out of the tap

Marion Water Division draws on groundwater from seven sources, six in Rochester and one in Marion, plus water from the Mattapoisett River Valley Water District. The district page says the area's wells have high iron and manganese and that a treatment plant on Tinkham Lane in Mattapoisett went online in spring 2008. Brown or orange water at one fixture is more likely a rusty pipe in your own house, so have a plumber check the supply line first.

The town also adjusts the water chemistry with potassium hydroxide to reduce corrosion. If you see staining or reduced flow only on the hot side, the water heater tank is the next place to look.

Sewer on most streets, septic on about a third of properties

Marion's wastewater plan counts roughly 32 miles of sewer, about 1,680 connections and around 900 properties on on-site septic, close to a third of developed lots. Tabor Academy on Front Street adds to the flows. If you are on the sewer, a main-line blockage may sit in your lateral or in the town line, and drain cleaning with a camera shows which side of the property line it is on.

On septic, a Title 5 inspection is required within two years before a sale, and a failed system generally must be upgraded within two years. Recurring backups before a listing are worth sorting out before the buyer's inspector finds them.

Plumbing permits in Marion

The Marion Building Department says homeowners cannot pull plumbing or gas permits, and that a licensed plumber or gas fitter must pull them and call the inspection. A gas water heater needs both a plumbing permit and a gas permit. The town lists inspections as usually done within 24 hours of the request on afternoons Monday through Thursday, so ask the department about timing on your job.

Permit rules behind a Marion call

Marion's Building Department sets the local steps, and 248 CMR 3.05 sets the state rule: no permit to clear a stoppage or repair a faucet, a permit before most other plumbing work. A licensed plumber pulls it, which is worth knowing before you hire anyone.
